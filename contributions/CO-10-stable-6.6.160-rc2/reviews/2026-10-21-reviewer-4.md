## Fit with Linux stable rules and successful upstream practice

This contribution is procedurally on very solid ground.

The official stable documentation describes precisely this workflow: patches enter the stable queue, undergo review, are posted again as an `-rc`, developers and testers test that `-rc`, and testers may respond on the mailing list with a `Tested-by:` email and testing information. If problems are found, patches can be modified or dropped and another `-rc` can be issued.

That means your email is **not unsolicited noise around an already-decided release**. Testing feedback is one of the intended inputs to the stable release decision. It directly serves the process the stable maintainers established.

The official definition of `Tested-by` is also important to your QCA9377 concern. It signifies that a person successfully tested a patch **in some environment**; it is deliberately not a certification covering all architectures, configurations, devices, workloads, or hardware. Thus an x86_64 QEMU/KVM environment using BlueZ virtual controllers is a legitimate basis for a `Tested-by`, provided the email accurately describes that environment—which version 3 now does.

### How your reply compares with successful stable-review replies

Established stable-review responses vary enormously in detail. In the 6.12.82-rc1 review, Mark Brown supplied essentially just a `Tested-by`; Harshit Mogalapalli said no problems were seen in x86_64/aarch64 testing; Shuah Khan reported compile/boot and no dmesg regression; Miguel Ojeda explicitly reported QEMU testing of several architectures while also mentioning known expected warnings; Jon Hunter supplied build, boot and test totals plus an exact kernel revision. All were normal contributions to the stable review.

Your proposed response therefore sits comfortably within upstream precedent. It is **more informative than the one-line responses but much smaller than a CI report**, and every extra line is doing useful work: it identifies the environment, demonstrates four concrete fixes, gives the most important regression totals, discloses the same-kernel L2CAP hang, and supplies the tag.

The fact that yours is QEMU-based is not a weakness in form. Miguel Ojeda’s stable-testing response, for example, explicitly reported QEMU boot tests and still supplied a `Tested-by`. Your environment actually goes substantially further than a boot test because it exercises Bluetooth kernel behavior through purpose-built emulator tests.

### What rejected/problematic stable work teaches

There is not really a meaningful category of “rejected `Tested-by` emails.” Stable test reports are evidence; what may be rejected or removed are the patches they reveal to be problematic. The official rules state that an objection or newly discovered issue can cause a patch to be dropped from the stable queue.

A useful concrete example comes from the 6.6.90-rc2 cycle. Testing found reproducible RISC-V build failures under multiple configurations/toolchains. Greg Kroah-Hartman concluded that the change was questionable for old kernels and caused build warnings, so he dropped it and asked maintainers to supply a working backport if they still wanted it.

That example illustrates what high-value testing looks like:

**specific kernel + specific configuration + reproducibility + exact failure + comparison to the expected state + an immediately understandable conclusion.**

Your work follows that model unusually well. The BT-9 test gives 3/3 failing on 6.6.159 and 3/3 passing on rc2. BT-8 gives the exact status byte before and after. The L2CAP difference is repeated 3/3. The `mgmt-tester` comparison is done case-by-case rather than merely comparing aggregate totals.

There is another maintainer lesson worth taking extremely seriously. During a 2026 stable discussion, Greg explicitly objected when maintainers had to reconstruct what should happen by digging through several old email threads, saying that approach does not scale with their mail volume; he requested a properly marked, self-contained resubmission instead.

Your upstream email is good precisely because it **does not require Greg or another maintainer to inspect your GitHub repository in order to understand the result**. The repository is excellent supporting evidence, but the essential result is in the message itself. That is exactly the right division of labor for a busy maintainer.

## Technical quality and correctness of the evidence

The strongest aspect of this contribution is not how many tests were run. It is **how the experiment is structured**.

You built the exact stable candidate `c578c158a36845a2b642bd491adaa7e4a479e04f` and the released 6.6.159 control, using BlueZ's `doc/tester.config`, `make olddefconfig`, and GCC 13.3.0. The results preserve the kernel image SHA-256 hashes, BlueZ tester revision information, QEMU/KVM setup, execution times, and test binaries. Kernel.org independently identifies 6.6.159 as the released 6.6 long-term kernel immediately preceding this review cycle, dated October 15, 2026.

This exact revision identification is more useful than it may appear. The stable documentation warns that `linux-stable-rc` is a snapshot of the stable queue, changes frequently, and is rebased often; it exists specifically for testing. Putting `c578c158a368` in the email therefore prevents future ambiguity over *which* 6.6.160-rc2 state was actually exercised. That is excellent practice.

The generated configs now publicly demonstrate that both builds actually enabled `CONFIG_KASAN=y`, `CONFIG_PROVE_LOCKING=y`, `CONFIG_LOCKDEP=y`, and `CONFIG_DEBUG_ATOMIC_SLEEP=y`. This matches BlueZ's published tester configuration, which contains KASAN, lockdep/prove-locking, debug atomic-sleep support, Bluetooth and VHCI-related configuration used for this form of testing.

That closes a subtle reproducibility hole present in earlier versions: you no longer merely say “we requested KASAN”; you document the resulting configuration.

### The BT-9 result is technically well grounded

The upstream BT-9 commit describes a race in simultaneous/interleaved discovery: if Inquiry Complete occurs between LE scan disable and the completion handling, discovery can remain stuck in `DISCOVERY_FINDING`; the fix sets the correct stopped state under the relevant conditions.

Your targeted BlueZ test fails three out of three times on 6.6.159 with Start Discovery answered `Busy`, while the same test passes three out of three on rc2; the corresponding normal-order control passes on both. The complete `mgmt-tester` result changes by exactly one verdict: `Start Discovery - Simultaneous Inquiry Late`, from Failed to Passed.

That is excellent evidence because it combines:

the negative control, the positive result, repetition, the full-suite context, and an earlier patch-isolation experiment documented in the dossier.

It is much stronger than simply observing that Bluetooth “seems to work.”

### The BT-8 result is almost textbook before/after evidence

The upstream BT-8 commit itself explains that the bad path can return status `0x00` (Success) when it should return the caller-provided status, including `MGMT_STATUS_NOT_POWERED`; its own virtual-controller test describes `0x00` before the fix and `0x0f` after it.

Your stable-candidate test reproduces exactly that distinction: 6.6.159 produces `0x00`, while rc2 gives `0x0f (Not Powered)`, two out of two in each state.

That causal correspondence is unusually clear. There is very little interpretation being imposed on an ambiguous test outcome.

### The incidental mesh and L2CAP confirmations are real signal, not padding

The upstream mesh fix explicitly describes both failures you see on 6.6.159: KASAN stack out-of-bounds in `set_mesh_sync` and a separate crash related to the `mgmt_pending_valid`/`mgmt_pending_remove` sequence. Your control kernel produces a KASAN stack-out-of-bounds in `set_mesh_sync` followed by a general protection fault involving `mgmt_pending_remove`; rc2's corresponding enable cases run cleanly.

Likewise, the upstream L2CAP fix explains an ECRED case where a DDDDND sequence of connections can exceed the intended channel maximum and write one `__le16` out of bounds. Your `Defer limit` case fails 3/3 on 6.6.159 and passes 3/3 on rc2.

Including those results is worthwhile. They give the stable maintainers independent runtime confirmation of **other authors' fixes in the same release candidate**, not just your own work. That makes the contribution more community-oriented and less like an attempt merely to obtain credit for your own patches.

### The broad regression pass is unusually thoughtful

For `mgmt-tester`, the control produced 464 passed, 38 failed, 6 timed out; rc2 produced 465 passed, 37 failed, 6 timed out. The only per-test verdict change is the intended BT-9 improvement.

For the remaining BlueZ CI TestRunner set, ISO, SCO, BNEP, RFCOMM, ioctl, SMP, user-channel and 6LoWPAN have identical verdict sets on both kernels. L2CAP is identical over the 96 cases completed by both except for the intended `Defer limit` improvement.

This suite selection is defensible. A published bluetooth-next CI run lists the TestRunner jobs for `6lowpan-tester`, `l2cap-tester`, `mesh-tester`, `mgmt-tester`, `sco-tester`, `bnep-tester`, `ioctl-tester`, `iso-tester`, `rfcomm-tester`, `smp-tester`, and `userchan-tester`—the same set your dossier covers when `mgmt-tester` is combined with `all-testers.sh`. BlueZ also describes `test-runner` as a tool for testing Linux Bluetooth kernel changes in a virtual machine.

I would nevertheless make the wording in the email more exact. BlueZ's source also builds programs named `gap-tester` and `hci-tester`, while they are not among that published TestRunner CI job set. Therefore:

> “ran BlueZ's emulator testers”

is understandable, but

> **“ran the BlueZ CI TestRunner suites”**

is harder to misinterpret and more precisely describes what you actually selected.

That is a very small wording improvement, not a methodological defect.

### The kernel-report checking is now strong

Version 3 adds a wider scanner covering oops/panic patterns, BUG/WARNING/cut-here lines, KASAN, UBSAN, KCSAN, several lockdep forms, atomic-sleep reports, RCU stalls, hung tasks, list/refcount corruption, general protection faults and call traces. All 18 rc2 logs are clean, while the same scanner detects the known 6.6.159 mesh failure as a positive control.

The positive control matters. A statement such as “grep found nothing” is weaker if there is no evidence that the check would notice a problem. Demonstrating that the same scanner detects the old kernel's known crash gives meaningful confidence that the rc2 result is not merely a broken reporting pipeline.

The proposed sentence—

> “No kernel warnings or sanitizer reports on rc2 in these runs.”

—is therefore appropriately scoped and supported.

## Remaining gaps and the highest-value final work

There is one issue I consider more important than all further polishing combined.

### Resolve the two mesh `Send cancel` cases

The full 6.6.159 mesh run crashes in `Mesh - Enable 2`; the rc2 run gets through that fixed case and later reaches two `Send cancel` tests, both of which time out. Consequently, those two rc2 timeouts have **no corresponding verdict from the full 6.6.159 run**. The results document says this explicitly.

This does **not** show a regression. The old kernel simply never reaches the tests. Your dossier also has evidence that the cases depend on newer upstream work not carried by stable.

But it does mean that the v3 sentence

> “No new failures in those tests”

is fractionally stronger than the direct A/B evidence warrants.

Fortunately, your own tooling provides a clean way to settle it. `repeat-case.sh` can run a tester with a one-word `-s` selector independently on 6.6.159 and rc2. The highest-return remaining experiment is therefore conceptually:

```text
repeat-case.sh <fresh-output-dir> mesh-tester cancel 3
```

I would do that **before any more broad testing**.

There are three useful outcomes:

| Result | Meaning | Upstream consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Both kernels time out identically | Strong confirmation they are pre-existing/version-skew behavior | Current green conclusion becomes substantially stronger |
| Both behave identically but intermittently | Flaky/non-regressive test behavior | Report only if needed; still no new stable regression |
| 6.6.159 passes while rc2 repeatedly times out | Candidate regression discovered | Do not send the current green wording; report the difference immediately |

That experiment is more valuable than running the already-green BT-8 or BT-9 reproducer ten more times.

### The L2CAP tail is optional, not a blocker

The L2CAP suite reaches 96 cases on each kernel and then both hang at the same `L2CAP Ext-Flowctl Server - Accept 2 Close 1` point; cases later in the suite consequently do not execute on either kernel. The email already says this explicitly.

For **absolute maximum coverage**, you could list the remaining cases and run them through targeted filters so the common hanging case no longer blocks the tail. That would convert “same through the common hang” into broader suite coverage.

I do **not** regard this as necessary before sending. The current disclosure is honest, both kernels stop in the same place, and stable-review testing has diminishing returns. The mesh cancel comparison is much higher priority because it addresses an actual set of rc2 outcomes for which the control verdict is currently absent.

The published bluetooth-next CI job set matches your suite inventory, whereas the BlueZ source tree contains some additional tester executables that are not part of those CI TestRunner jobs.

This change costs no space and makes the scope technically exact.

### Clean the public dossier before freezing it

Its top says testing is complete and v3 is ready, but a later section still says “Running: every other BlueZ tester,” and `## Next` still instructs you to fill the other tester result and replace a `[PENDING]` line that no longer exists. Likewise, the current `REVIEW-REQUEST.md` correctly points reviewers to v3 but still summarizes the whole result with “no regressions,” even though v3 deliberately replaced the equivalent language with more precise scoping.

**These do not make the upstream email worse.** Greg does not need to read either file. But they matter under your own higher standard: a future person entering the dossier should encounter one consistent state, not have to determine which paragraph is obsolete.

For a polished final artifact, I would update those two files, then record the exact repository commit corresponding to the sent email. Since your whole project emphasizes evidence provenance, freezing “what exactly was sent” is worthwhile.

Successful stable-review replies routinely report much narrower coverage. The point is to provide a reliable new signal, not maximize the amount of work performed.

## Final quality assessment

The underlying contribution is already **substantively excellent**.

What makes it unusually good is not the amount of documentation. It is that the work has several layers of independent cross-checking:

the exact rc is frozen by SHA despite the rebasing nature of `linux-stable-rc`;  
the immediately previous released kernel is the control;  
the two project-originated behavioral fixes have purpose-built targeted tests;  
the entire 508-case management suite is compared by test identity rather than just pass totals;  
the rest of the BlueZ CI TestRunner set is compared between kernels;  
the two unexpected positive improvements correspond closely to their upstream fixes;  
kernel diagnostics are scanned with a positive control proving the scanner catches a real crash;  
the generated configurations and build inputs are now published;  
and the actual maintainer-facing email remains short and self-contained instead of transferring your verification burden to Greg or the Bluetooth maintainers.

That last point is especially important. Kernel contribution quality is not proportional to how much evidence you force a maintainer to read. Greg has explicitly warned that making maintainers reconstruct intent by digging through threads does not scale. Your dossier can be extensive because **you** carry that complexity; the stable maintainer receives the distilled result. That is good community engineering.

The present weak points are small and mostly at the polish boundary rather than the engineering core: the unmatched mesh `Send cancel` outcomes, slightly ambiguous “BlueZ's emulator testers” terminology, an underpowered mail-header linter, and stale state text in the public README/review request.

So my final judgment is:

**Useful to Linux stable:** yes, clearly.  
**Useful to Bluetooth:** yes, strongly.  
**Within the rules:** yes.  
**Consistent with successful stable-review practice:** yes.  
**Likely to burden maintainers:** no; it should reduce uncertainty rather than create work.

**Would I send the current v3 unchanged?** It would already be a defensible contribution, but **not quite under your requested “top-quality from every angle” standard**.  
**What changes that verdict?** Run the isolated mesh `cancel` comparison, use “BlueZ CI TestRunner suites” and “directly comparable completed cases,” clean the two stale dossier summaries, verify the raw threading/recipient headers, and freeze the final sent state.

With those small final steps, I would regard this as **exemplary stable-release testing work**: the kind of contribution that gives maintainers additional confidence without asking them to redo the tester's homework.
